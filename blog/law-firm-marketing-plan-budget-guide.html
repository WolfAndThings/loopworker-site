Law firm marketing plans and budgets at mid-size firms

What firms spend on marketing and business development, how much support lawyers get, and which activities marketing leaders say are worth the effort.

October 20, 2026 · Alex Lamb

What firms spend on marketing

BTI Consulting Group reported in The Mad Clientist that marketing and business development spend as a percent of revenue was 2.36% in 2023 and a projected 2.47% in 2024. In 2018 it was 2.76%.

These are averages across firm sizes. No free US source gives a share of revenue for mid-size firms alone. A mid-size firm that benchmarks against 2.36% is comparing itself to a blended figure.

Where the money goes has changed too. BTI reports that 57% of the budget now goes to client development. Before the pandemic, client development took 34%.

Two measures that point different ways

The sources do not line up neatly. BTI's figure is spend as a share of revenue, and it sits below its 2018 level. The 2025 Legal Marketing Decision-Makers Survey from the Legal Marketing Association and Above the Law asked about budgets after inflation. Jeremy Barker of Above the Law reported that 54% of respondents said budgets rose in recent years, and 14% said they fell.

They measure different things: one is a share of revenue, the other is a budget adjusted for inflation. Read them side by side, not as a single trend.

Firm size splits the survey. 74% of leaders at firms with 51 to 100 attorneys said budgets had increased, compared with 44% at firms with more than 500 attorneys. Marketers at firms with 251 to 500 attorneys were the most likely to report smaller budgets. The survey had nearly 100 self-reported respondents, so these splits are directional.

Staffing support at mid-size firms

BTI's 2025 benchmarking of budgets and staffing found midsize and smaller firms give attorneys less marketing and business development support than before the pandemic. Am Law 30 firms were the only group to increase attorney support levels.

BTI also reports that only 1 in 5 law firms have dedicated digital marketers.

Outside help covers some of the gap. In the LMA and Above the Law survey, a majority said their firms use outside vendors for marketing technology, and most use outside vendors for media relations.

The decision for a mid-size firm: does the plan assume in-house digital skill the team may not have, and which work belongs with vendors.

What marketing leaders say works

In the LMA and Above the Law survey, a majority of marketing leaders ranked written content development as the single most important marketing activity. Branding and profile building came next.

Directories and awards sat at the other end. A majority of respondents ranked them the least useful activity.

On business development, a majority ranked coaching lawyers on business development and gathering client feedback as the most important activities.

Marketing leaders also sit closer to firm strategy than the budget line suggests. A majority of those surveyed take part directly in their firm's strategic planning, and almost 20% say they are a primary author of the plan.

Where spending is moving

Isaac Brooks of the Thomson Reuters Institute named business development and marketing, along with knowledge management and technology, as the main high-growth expense categories. Am Law 200 firms led that growth.

Midsize firms trail on part of it. Zoe Miranda of the Thomson Reuters Institute reported that in Q1 2026, midsize firms grew spending on technology and knowledge management by 6.2%, the lowest increase of any segment Thomson Reuters tracks.

Calibrate Legal's study of Am Law 200 firms found that nearly 85% of marketing and business development effort falls outside direct or high-value revenue influence. That study covers Am Law 200 firms, not mid-size firms.

For a mid-size marketing leader, the open questions are where to set spend against the BTI average, how far to shift toward client development, and which effort can be tied to revenue.
